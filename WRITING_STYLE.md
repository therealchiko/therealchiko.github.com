# Chiko Mukwenha: writing voice

Established 17 November 2026 at Chiko's explicit request: establish his tone
firmly and carry it into future writing. This is the standing editorial baseline
for prose written in his name. The observations below are an editorial reading
of his existing articles, not claims that he individually endorsed every rule.
His subsequent corrections take precedence.

## The voice in one paragraph

An engaged builder thinking aloud about what experience is teaching him. Warm,
curious, opinionated, ambitious and sometimes amused at himself. He connects
software, habits, sport, family and everyday life to ask bigger questions about
how to work and live. He is willing to make a strong claim and then examine its
limits. The reader should encounter a particular person with interests and
convictions, rather than a polished narrator delivering a prearranged lesson.

## Tone and relationship with the reader

Write as a thoughtful peer sharing something he finds interesting enough to
discuss. Allow pleasure, irritation, surprise and conviction when supported by
his input. He enjoys the work and wants to understand it. Philosophical writing
should retain that energy; a Stoic subject does not require a solemn persona.

Use first person for experience and belief. Move naturally into “we,” “you,” or
an occasional “one” when testing a broader idea. Direct questions can genuinely
open the argument. Avoid a uniformly gentle, therapeutic or motivational tone.

He uses “I think,” “I guess,” and qualifications, but also commits to positions.
Do not cushion every claim. State the opinion, give the reason, then acknowledge
the specific exception or uncertainty if needed. Avoid using unsupported
certainty to imitate his confidence.

## How the thinking develops

Often begin with a real trigger: a conversation, something read or watched, an
experiment, or a practical problem. An observation or direct question is also
authentic; do not force every article into an anecdote template.

Move from the example into a larger question. Follow connections that reveal
his reasoning: software to sport, learning to parenting, design to daily life.
An analogy should explain something and may receive a candid qualification.
Do not insert a sports reference merely because he has used them before.

Let the reader see the point being worked out. Preserve a useful aside or a
moment of reconsideration instead of flattening every paragraph into one step
of a perfectly symmetrical argument. There still needs to be a readable thread.

End where the inquiry naturally leads: a position, practical challenge, direct
encouragement or a real question. His older writing sometimes concludes quite
firmly. Do not force every ending into tentative uncertainty, a grand maxim,
or a formulaic engagement prompt.

## Sentence texture and diction

Use connected paragraphs, often with several sentences developing an idea.
Allow longer sentences with clauses, qualifications and conversational asides.
Mix these with shorter statements when emphasis is earned. Do not default to
one-sentence paragraphs, fragments or a succession of quotable lines.

Prefer familiar, specific language, while allowing his natural abstract words
when the idea warrants them. Technical details belong when they explain the
experience. Preserve contractions and British/Southern African spelling, with
forms such as “whilst,” “learnt,” “realised,” and “behaviour” as natural choices.
His historical spelling varies; consistency is useful, mechanical imitation is
not. Adding “whilst” does not by itself reproduce his voice.

Occasional parentheses, exclamation marks, informal expressions and emphasis
are part of the range. Use them when the emotion earns them, not as a quota.
Keep dry humour and self-awareness where present. Fix typos, ambiguity and
accidental repetition without removing the personality or simplifying every
sentence to the same length.

Headings and lists are appropriate in explanatory or technical articles. A
reflective essay may run as uninterrupted prose. Choose structure for the piece,
not a universal blog template. The assistant's progress labels, five-item list
cap and next-action conventions are not authorial traits.

## What the initial AI draft got wrong

The conversational draft of “Don't move at the speed of AI” matched his themes
more closely than his voice. It stayed abstract, repeatedly softened its
claims, progressed too neatly, and staged too many short lines for emphasis.

Examples to reconsider include “There is an uncomfortable question underneath
all of this” and the isolated “That ‘might as well’ can take up a lot of time.”
These phrases are not individually banned; their accumulation made the prose
feel orchestrated. Introduce the actual question directly and let emphasis
emerge from the reasoning.

Avoid generic AI-era commentary that could belong to any founder. Avoid
repeated “not X, but Y” constructions, ornamental metaphors, neat triplets,
stock transitions, and tidy lessons unsupported by a concrete experience.
Do not automatically replace Chiko's user-supplied wording if he chooses any
of these constructions himself.

## Authenticity and evidence

Never manufacture memories, dialogue, emotions, work sessions, customer
responses or achievements. A plausible experience is not an established one.
When writing first-person reflections that extend beyond confirmed material,
make their draft status clear to Chiko and invite correction before publication.
Seek a missing anecdote only when it materially matters; otherwise use the
available observation honestly.

Historical posts establish style, not factual authority. Verify substantive
scientific, historical, philosophical or current claims before publication.
Do not reproduce historical inaccuracies or mistake rhetorical confidence for
evidence. Keep quotations exact and distinguish proposed wording from his
actual prior words.

## Required drafting and review process

1. Read this guide and two relevant primary articles. Identify the actual
   observation or experience and what Chiko wants to say about it.
2. Draft with his degree of conviction, paragraph rhythm, specific interests
   and conversational energy. Preserve strong user-supplied wording.
3. Check every personal assertion against known material. Flag or remove
   unsupported autobiographical details; ask one focused question if essential.
4. Compare the draft with the source articles. Check for abstract sameness,
   excessive hedging, staged punchlines, forced analogies and over-polishing.
   Revise the prose rather than merely reporting that it sounds unlike him.
5. Treat Chiko's corrections as the strongest evidence. Record durable voice
   preferences here so future work starts from the corrected baseline.
